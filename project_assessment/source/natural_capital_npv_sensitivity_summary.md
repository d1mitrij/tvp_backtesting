# When Nature Has a Price Tag: Understanding How Natural Resources Affect a Company's Financial Value

*A plain-language summary of the Natural Capital NPV Sensitivity Framework*

---

## The Big Idea in One Sentence

If a company's business depends on a natural resource — water, clean air, healthy land, a stable climate — and that resource is degrading or becoming regulated or contested in court, the company's financial returns will shrink, sometimes enough to wipe out its profits entirely.

---

## Why This Matters Now

For a long time, businesses could use natural resources — water from a river, the atmosphere as a dumping ground for emissions, fertile soil, clean air — without paying for them. Those costs were absorbed by nature, by local communities, or by future generations. They did not appear on any balance sheet.

Three things are changing simultaneously, and they are changing fast:

**1. Resources are physically running out or degrading.**
Rivers run dry in drought years. Soils lose fertility. Extreme heat shuts down outdoor operations. A farm that used to have reliable water now faces seasons where the river simply cannot supply enough to grow a full crop. The resource that was free and abundant is now scarce and sometimes absent.

**2. Governments are putting a price on resource use.**
Carbon taxes, water abstraction fees, biodiversity net gain requirements, deforestation bans — these are already law in many jurisdictions and spreading fast. When a government sets a price on something a company previously used for free, that price becomes a new cost that directly reduces profit margins.

**3. Courts are assigning liability for past resource use.**
This is the newest and least anticipated channel. A landmark 2024 paper published in the journal *Science* by Oxford researchers Wetzer, Stuart-Smith, and Dibley shows that climate litigation is growing rapidly and that investors are "flying blind" to the financial risk it creates. Using attribution science — which can now link specific emissions from specific companies to specific measurable damages — courts can and do hold companies responsible for the harm their historical resource use has caused. Chevron alone, for example, could face liability of up to $8.5 trillion from climate lawsuits — nearly 30 times its total profits over the previous three decades. The researchers conclude that Chevron's business "may in fact be net value-destroying."

These three pressures do not work independently. When they all hit a company at once, the financial damage is far worse than the sum of its parts.

---

## How a Small Resource Problem Becomes a Large Financial Problem

Here is the mechanism in plain terms.

Imagine a company that sells a product. Its profit on each unit sold is the difference between what it charges (the price) and what it costs to make (the cost). This is called the **contribution margin** — the money each sale contributes toward covering the fixed costs of running the business (rent, loan repayments, staff salaries that cannot be cut quickly).

Now add natural capital costs to the picture. The company uses water to make its product. Water was free. Now it is not. That cost now reduces the contribution margin.

If the margin was already thin — say the company earns €2 per unit after conventional costs, against €500,000 in fixed costs — then:
- At current volume (100,000 units), it earns exactly enough: 100,000 × €2 = €200,000 profit after fixed costs
- If the water price rises and margin drops to €1.50, it now needs to sell 133,000 units just to break even — more than it may be able to produce

This is called the **breakeven shift**: the minimum number of units the company must sell to avoid a loss rises sharply when input costs increase. And because fixed costs do not move — the loans still need to be repaid, the factory still needs to be heated — every euro of margin compression requires a proportionally large increase in sales volume to compensate. The smaller the margin, the worse the amplification.

The technical term for this amplification is **operating leverage**. Businesses with high fixed costs and thin margins are most exposed. A coal-fired power station, a cement factory, a textile mill using river water, an agribusiness in a water-stressed basin — all of these have high fixed costs relative to their margins and therefore high exposure when their natural resource inputs become expensive or unavailable.

## The Three Channels, in Plain Language

### Channel 1: Physical — The resource becomes unavailable

A river dries up. A harvest fails because pollinators have disappeared. A site must close because heat stress makes outdoor work impossible. The company produces less, so it earns less, but still has to pay its fixed costs. Profits collapse — sometimes to zero.

### Channel 2: Transition — The government puts a price on the resource

A carbon price is introduced. Water abstraction fees are raised. A new law requires companies to restore the biodiversity they disturb. These costs are real and immediate. They reduce the contribution margin on every unit the company sells. If the company cannot raise its prices to customers (because its competitors, or market conditions, prevent it), the entire cost increase comes out of profit. And as shown above, even a small margin reduction can require a large volume increase to compensate.

The key word here is **transition**: companies that see this coming and plan for it — by switching energy sources, reducing water use, restoring land — can manage the transition gradually. Companies that do not plan for it face the full cost at once, often at the worst possible moment.

### Channel 3: Legal — Courts hold the company responsible for past harm

This is the newest channel, and in many ways the most powerful. It works like this: if scientists can now demonstrate (and they increasingly can) that a specific company's emissions, or water extraction, or land clearance caused quantifiable harm to specific people or ecosystems, then those people or governments can sue. The social cost of that harm — the cost of the damage done — can be large, sometimes exceeding the company's entire profit history.

The three channels interact. A company whose resource is physically degrading may also face transition pricing on that same resource *and* litigation from people harmed by that degradation — all at the same time. The financial damage compounds rather than adds.

## The Two Ways Out

A company's financial sensitivity to any specific natural resource can be **reduced** in one of two ways:

### Way 1: The company has a verified plan to reduce its dependency

This means an independently checked, time-bound, measurable commitment to use less of the resource — or to use it differently. Examples:
- A Science-Based Target for emissions reduction (not just a pledge — a verified trajectory)
- A water management plan under ISO standards, with documented recycling and reduced abstraction
- A biodiversity management plan with measured restoration outcomes
- A supply chain audit for living wages and labour rights

The plan must be genuine. Vague commitments without milestones, independent verification, or measurable targets do not reduce financial exposure — they may actually increase legal exposure if courts view them as evidence of awareness without action.

### Way 2: The country or location where the company operates has a binding plan to restore the resource

Some of the risk is not the company's alone to manage. If the government has committed — credibly and bindingly — to restore the water basin, increase forest cover, improve air quality, or put a trajectory on carbon pricing, then all companies operating in that jurisdiction benefit from the systemic improvement. Their resource security improves; the regulatory risk becomes foreseeable; the legal risk is attenuated because policy action breaks the causal chain between historical use and future harm.

This is what a well-constructed national climate commitment (NDC — Nationally Determined Contribution) does. It is not just a diplomatic document. For companies and investors, a credible NDC for the relevant sector is a form of natural capital management hedge.

---

## Acting Early vs. Acting Late: The Difference Is Enormous

The timing of action — by companies or by the countries where they operate — determines whether the financial impact is manageable or catastrophic.

**If the risk is addressed early:**

The company faces rising costs on a known, predictable schedule. It can plan its investments, pass some costs through to customers before competitors do, reduce its dependence gradually, and hedge appropriately. The financial hit is real but spread over time and proportional to the pace of change. This is what the framework calls **transition risk** — uncomfortable but manageable.

**If the risk is not addressed and materialises later:**

The physical degradation arrives suddenly (a drought year, a heat wave, a regulatory ban). Production falls. Costs spike. The legal flywheel — described by the Oxford researchers — has already spun up: money has flowed into climate litigation, cases have been won, attribution science is mature, and your sector is now a target. The company faces all three channels simultaneously, with no hedging in place, at a moment when its margins are already compressed by the physical shock.

The financial difference between these two pathways is not 10% or 20% — it can be the difference between a profitable business and one whose accumulated liabilities exceed its entire value.

## Which Sectors Are Most Exposed?

The resources companies depend on vary by sector. The framework maps these dependencies across twelve industry categories:

| Who is most exposed | To what |
|---|---|
| Fossil fuels, utilities, transport | Carbon emissions, energy costs, air quality |
| Agriculture, food and drink | Water availability, land and soil health, biodiversity |
| Mining, cement, chemicals | All of the above, plus waste and contamination |
| Textiles and apparel | Water and living wages |
| Construction and real estate | Land, biodiversity, energy efficiency |
| Banks and financial institutions | Not directly — but through the companies they lend to |

For banks and investors, the point is important: the legal liability does not stop at the company that uses the resource. It can travel up the ownership chain. A lender that finances a water-intensive operation in a water-stressed basin may find the loan impaired not because of the borrower's financial mismanagement, but because the basin dried up.

---

## What a Company Can Show to Reduce Its Financial Exposure

The framework uses a simple checklist logic. For each natural resource a company depends on, the question is:

1. **Is the resource genuinely material to the business?** If not, it is not a priority.
2. **Is an adverse scenario actively developing?** (Physical stress, incoming regulation, growing litigation in this sector or indicator?)
3. **Does the company have a verified management plan** to reduce its dependency on this specific resource?
4. **Does the location have a binding plan** to restore or increase the availability of this specific resource?
5. **Has the dependency been addressed in the relevant national climate commitment (NDC)?**

If yes to 3 or 4, the financial sensitivity is reduced — not eliminated, but reduced.
If no to both 3 and 4, the company carries the full sensitivity. How that sensitivity is classified — transition or physical risk — depends on whether anyone is planning for it.

---

## The Upside: Positive Sensitivity

Not all natural capital dependencies are negative. The same framework that identifies liabilities also identifies assets.

A company that:
- Creates stable, well-paying local jobs (employment multiplier)
- Generates clean energy that displaces health-damaging air pollution
- Restores water quality or increases green cover
- Reduces emissions below a credible pathway

creates measurable positive externalities. Under the Transitionvaluation framework developed by Greenings, the Value Balancing Alliance, and WifOR, these positive impacts increase the project's total value beyond its conventional financial return.

In practice, this means projects with genuine positive natural capital contributions can issue **green bonds at lower interest rates** — roughly 0.15–0.20% below conventional infrastructure debt — because the verified reduction in risk and verified creation of societal value translates into a lower risk premium for investors.

A company that has eliminated its material natural capital dependencies and generates net positive impacts achieves what the framework calls **ONPV > NPV** — its total opportunity value exceeds its conventional financial value. It is, in other words, a better investment than its financial accounts alone would suggest.

---

## Summary

| Situation | Financial outcome |
|---|---|
| Company depends on natural resource; no plan; no country plan | Full exposure — transition risk will become physical and legal risk |
| Company has verified plan to reduce dependency | Reduced sensitivity; manageable transition risk |
| Country has binding plan to restore resource biocapacity | Systemic protection; residual risk still tracked |
| Both company plan AND country plan in place | Low sensitivity; green bond eligible; ONPV premium |
| No action, scenario materialises | Contribution margin collapses; breakeven rises above production capacity; NPV turns negative; legal liability accumulates |

> The window to convert what will be physical and legal risk into manageable transition risk is open now — but it closes as the physical degradation accelerates and as litigation builds momentum. A 2024 paper in *Science* found that 2,485 climate lawsuits have already been filed globally, that claimants are becoming more successful, and that the capital funding litigation is growing. Companies and countries that act early, with credible and verified plans, face a manageable repricing of their natural capital dependencies. Those that do not will face the same costs — but concentrated, sudden, compounded, and increasingly pursued through the courts.
